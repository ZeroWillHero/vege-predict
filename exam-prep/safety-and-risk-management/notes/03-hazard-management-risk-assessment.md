## 1. OHS Hazard Management

**Definition:** a **systematic process** to protect workers from potential harm, ensuring safe and healthful working conditions — a **structured approach to identifying, evaluating and controlling** hazards that could cause injuries, illnesses or incidents. Its core is its **proactive nature**. (OSHA: effective controls protect workers, prevent incidents, reduce risks and support employers.)

### ⭐ Q1b — 5 benefits for EMPLOYERS (5 marks, learn verbatim)
1. **Reduces the risk of accidents and injuries** — proactive management minimises workplace dangers
2. **Improves efficiency and productivity** — fewer accidents = less disruption and absenteeism
3. **Boosts employee relations and morale** — safer environment → job satisfaction and retention
4. **Lowers costs associated with accidents** — healthcare costs, lost productivity, legal liabilities
5. **Ensures compliance with legal and regulatory requirements** — avoids penalties, protects brand image

Mnemonic: **R-I-B-L-E** → *"**R**educe, **I**mprove, **B**oost, **L**ower, **E**nsure."*

**Benefits for employees:** protection from harm; safer, less stressful environment; better well-being and job satisfaction.
**Benefits for the organisation:** demonstrates legal/ethical/moral responsibility · better performance monitoring · clear improvement objectives · enhances customer trust · significant cost savings.

## 2. Research methods for hazard assessment (the 5 tools)

### 2.1 Workplace Inspections ⭐ (Q2c & Q2d — 9 marks combined!)

**Main objectives (list 4):**
1. **Proactively identify hazards** (existing and potential)
2. **Uncover root causes** of hazards
3. **Recommend corrective actions**
4. **Evaluate the effectiveness of existing risk-control measures**
5. *(bonus)* Ensure compliance with safety standards

**Systematic procedure for an effective inspection (Q2d — 5 marks):**
1. **Plan & prepare** — define scope/area/frequency; review past inspection reports, incident records, SDS and equipment manuals
2. **Prepare a customised checklist** — tailored to that workplace's environment, equipment and processes; ensures thoroughness and consistency
3. **Involve workers** — they have direct knowledge of the tasks and risks; makes inspection more effective
4. **Conduct the walkthrough** — systematically observe operations, equipment and facilities; talk to operators; note unsafe acts and unsafe conditions
5. **Record findings** — document every hazard identified, with location, photos and risk rating
6. **Recommend and assign corrective actions** — with a responsible person and a deadline
7. **Follow up / re-inspect** — verify actions were completed and effective (accountability)
8. **Report & communicate results** — to management and workers; track trends over time

### 2.2 Incident Investigations
*Purpose:* determine the **sequence of events**, identify **root causes**, recommend corrective actions, prevent recurrence.
**Steps (Lecture-3 version — 7 steps):**
1. **Report** the incident to designated personnel
2. **Provide first aid / medical care** to injured parties
3. **Secure the scene** to preserve evidence
4. **Collect facts and data**
5. **Interview witnesses**
6. **Analyse** the information to pinpoint underlying causes
7. **Prepare a report** with findings and recommendations

> ⚠️ Lecture 1 gave a 6-step version (Secure → Gather → Analyse → Corrective actions → Report → Monitor). Either is acceptable; the 7-step one adds *report the incident* and *first aid* at the front.

### 2.3 Exposure Monitoring
Measuring & assessing workers' exposure to hazardous **substances, physical agents or biological agents**.
*Purpose:* understand **level and duration** of exposure and decide whether controls are needed.
*Techniques by route:* **Inhalation** hazards (personal air sampling pumps, direct-reading instruments) · **Ingestion** exposure (surface/hand wipe sampling, hygiene checks) · **Dermal** exposure (patch sampling, skin wipes).

### 2.4 Ergonomic Assessments ⭐ (Q1a-ii — 8 marks)
*Purpose:* identify and **quantify risk factors** that lead to **musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs)**; evaluate the worker↔environment interaction and find mismatches.

**Process (4 steps):**
1. **Review existing data** on workplace injuries and illnesses
2. **Gather subjective data** — workplace walk-throughs + employee feedback
3. **Collect objective data** using ergonomic assessment tools
4. **Analyse all data** — identify insights, prioritise risks, develop mitigation strategies

**The 4 tools (memorise the acronyms — easy marks):**
| Tool | What it is |
|---|---|
| **WISHA Checklist** | Washington State caution-zone screening checklist |
| **NIOSH Lifting Equation** | Calculates the Recommended Weight Limit for manual lifting |
| **REBA** | *Rapid Entire Body Assessment* — whole-body posture scoring |
| **RULA** | *Rapid Upper Limb Assessment* — neck, trunk, upper-limb scoring |

### 2.5 Qualitative vs Quantitative Risk Assessment ⭐

| | **Qualitative** | **Quantitative** |
|---|---|---|
| Basis | **Non-numerical** — descriptive judgements of severity & likelihood | **Numerical values** assigned to likelihood and severity |
| Output | Prioritised list of risks; High / Medium / Low categories | Financial impact figures, probabilities, expected loss |
| Main tool | **Risk matrix** | Calculations |
| Methods | FMEA, Business Impact Analysis (BIA), **RAG ratings** (Red-Amber-Green) | **Quantitative FMEA** (numerical ratings for **Occurrence, Severity, Detection** → RPN), **Quantitative BIA** (financial impact of disasters), **Expected Monetary Value (EMV)** |
| Strength | Fast, cheap, works with little data | Objective, comparable, supports cost-benefit decisions |
| Weakness | Subjective | Data-hungry, harder, may give false precision |

Process for both: **identify → classify → control → continuously monitor**.

## 3. The Risk Assessment Process (5 steps — the spine of the whole course)

```
1. Hazard Identification
2. Risk Evaluation  (Likelihood × Severity)
3. Risk Prioritisation  (risk matrix)
4. Implement Control Measures  (hierarchy of controls)
5. Documentation and Review
```

### Step 1 — Hazard Identification: 5 methods
1. Regular **workplace inspections**
2. Analysing **job descriptions** and physical/mental demands of roles
3. **Job Safety Analysis (JSA)** — break jobs into tasks, identify hazards per task
4. Reviewing **past incident records**, including **near misses**
5. Examining documents — **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** and **equipment manuals**

### Step 2 — Risk Evaluation
**Likelihood** = how probable it is the hazard causes injury/illness. Consider:
- Frequency of exposure · Number of people exposed · History of similar incidents · Effectiveness of existing controls

**Severity** = extent of damage if it happens; ranges from minor injury to fatality or major property damage. Assessed on qualitative scales: **negligible → minor → moderate → major → catastrophic**. Research the hazard, and consider work environment and worker capabilities.

### Step 3 — Risk Prioritisation: the **Risk Matrix**
A grid with **likelihood on one axis and severity on the other**. Hazards are plotted to read off the risk level; divided into categories (**low / medium / high / extreme**) with colour-coding.

```
Severity →      Negligible  Minor    Moderate   Major    Catastrophic
Likelihood ↓
Almost certain     M          H        H          E          E
Likely             L          M        H          H          E
Possible           L          M        M          H          E
Unlikely           L          L        M          M          H
Rare               L          L        L          M          H
     L=Low  M=Medium  H=High  E=Extreme
```

### Step 4 — Control Measures: **Hierarchy of Controls**
Elimination → Substitution → Engineering → Administrative → **PPE** (see Lecture 1 table). Prioritise higher-level controls whenever feasible.

### Step 5 — Documentation & Review
Documentation is needed for **legal compliance, communication, tracking progress and future assessments**. It must record:
- all identified hazards · evaluated risks (likelihood + severity) · selected control measures · **persons responsible** · **review dates**

Review **regularly**, and always when the workplace changes (new equipment, process, people, or after an incident).

## 4. Acceptable Risk ⭐ (Q3a — 2 marks, Q3b — 5 marks)

**Definition (Q3a):** the level of risk an organisation or society is **willing to tolerate**, given the benefits derived from the activity and the cost of further risk reduction. It is the likelihood of disease/injury tolerated by an individual, group or society — depending on scientific data, social norms, economic conditions and legal frameworks. It must fall within the organisation's **risk appetite and risk tolerance**.

- **Risk appetite** = how much risk the organisation *wants* to take to meet objectives.
- **Risk tolerance** = the acceptable *deviation* around that.

### Three factor groups shaping acceptability
| **Legal** | **Ethical** | **Economic** |
|---|---|---|
| Comply with national/local OSH law (sets the *minimum*) · industry-specific standards as benchmarks · employer's legal obligation to provide a safe workplace; failure → penalties and liability | Moral responsibility to protect employees & prioritise well-being/quality of life · balance risk vs benefit ethically — **"do no harm"** · cost-benefit analysis has ethical limits: must not endorse pain and suffering for economic reasons | Cost of controls vs potential financial losses from accidents · **cost-benefit analysis** determines when further reduction becomes **"grossly disproportionate"** · impacts individuals, organisations and society |

### ⭐ Q3b — 7 challenges in determining acceptable risk (name any 5)
1. **Subjectivity of risk perception** — different individuals/groups see risk differently
2. **Difficulty quantifying certain risks** — especially long-term health effects
3. **Balancing competing priorities** — production demands vs safety
4. **Uncertainty and limited knowledge** about potential hazards
5. **Dynamic nature of risk** and **evolving societal values**
6. **Lack of clear public debate/consensus** on specific risk levels
7. **External influences** — media coverage and public opinion

Mnemonic: **S-Q-B-U-D-L-E** → *"**S**ubjective **Q**uantification **B**alances **U**ncertain **D**ynamic **L**ay **E**xternalities."*

## 5. ALARP Principle ⭐ (Q3c-i — 4 marks)

**ALARP = As Low As Reasonably Practicable.**

- Means reducing risk to a level that is *as low as reasonably practicable* by **balancing the level of risk against the time, effort and cost** needed to reduce it further.
- The **"grossly disproportionate" test**: if the cost of further reducing the risk is *much greater* than the benefit gained, further measures are not reasonably practicable. (Note: the burden increases as the risk increases — the sacrifice must be *grossly*, not merely marginally, disproportionate.)
- ALARP decides what level of **residual risk** is acceptable after all reasonable steps.

**Three risk regions (draw the carrot/triangle diagram):**
```
        ▲  INTOLERABLE REGION
        │  Risk cannot be justified except in extraordinary
        │  circumstances → the activity must stop
        ├──────────────────────────────────
        │  ALARP / TOLERABLE REGION
        │  Risk tolerated only if reduction is impracticable
        │  or cost is grossly disproportionate to the benefit
        ├──────────────────────────────────
        │  BROADLY ACCEPTABLE REGION
        ▼  Risk is negligible; maintain existing controls
```
> ⚠️ Do not confuse **ALARP** (risk management, "practicable") with **ALARA** (radiation protection, "achievable" — Lecture 6).

## 8. Factors contributing to accidents (4 groups)

> Accidents are **rarely a single cause** — they arise from a complex interplay.

| Group | Contributing factors |
|---|---|
| **Human factors** | Fatigue, stress, lack of experience, inadequate training, poor supervision, personal problems, negligence, anger, hasty decisions, indifference, distraction, curiosity, poor work habits, overconfidence, lack of planning. ⚠️ Distinguish **human error** (unintended action) from **violation** (deliberate rule-breaking) |
| **Equipment / machinery** | Inadequate maintenance, defective machinery, unsafe practices, missing guards, design flaws, manufacturing errors, improper assembly. Hazards: crushing, entanglement, struck-by, tip-over, falls from equipment, transport accidents. *Prevention:* maintenance, inspections, follow manufacturer instructions, machine guarding |
| **Environmental** | Poor lighting · inadequate ventilation · extreme temperatures · slippery surfaces · excessive noise |
| **Organisational** | Lack of management commitment · inadequate staffing/training/supervision · poor communication · weak safety policies · **blame culture** · production-vs-safety pressure |

## 9. Safety Checklists
**Benefits (7):** systematic hazard identification & assessment · regulatory compliance · valuable **training tool** · promotes a **proactive** approach · facilitates communication & collaboration · tracks improvements and identifies trends · digital checklists add efficiency and real-time data.

**Designing effective checklists:** identify hazards specific to the workplace/task · organise into clear categories (general safety, equipment, emergency) · standardised format, clear concise language · consider digital · comprehensive coverage + regular updates · assign responsibility, leave space for comments/corrective actions · record who, when, where and the conditions.

## 10. Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) ⭐ (Q3c-ii — 4 marks)

**What it is:** a **top-down, deductive** method used in safety and reliability engineering to understand how systems can fail, identify risk-reduction methods and determine the probability of an accident/system failure.

**Purpose:** diagnose root causes of failures · determine inherent risks and how to minimise them · estimate the frequency of safety breaches.

**Key principles:**
- Based on **multi-causality** — uses a logic diagram to trace event branches
- Uses **Boolean logic** with **AND / OR** gates (and INHIBIT)
- Starts with an **undesired TOP event** and works **backwards** to the causes
- Applicable both **qualitatively** and **quantitatively**

**Constructing a fault tree:**
1. Define the undesired **top event** clearly and specifically
2. Identify all potential causes and contributing factors (needs system understanding)
3. Draw the diagram **top-down**, using event symbols (basic, intermediate, undeveloped, conditional) and logic gates (AND, OR, INHIBIT)

**Interpreting:**
- **Qualitative** — identify failure pathways and event combinations that cause the top event
- **Quantitative** — assign probabilities to basic events and calculate the top-event probability
- Key concepts: **Minimal Cut Sets (MCS)** — smallest sets of basic events that *together cause* the top event; **Minimal Path Sets (MPS)** — smallest sets that, if all work, *prevent* it

**Gate maths:** OR gate → P = P₁ + P₂ − P₁P₂ (≈ sum for small values). AND gate → P = P₁ × P₂.

**Simple example to draw in the exam:**
```
                 TOP EVENT: Worker receives electric shock
                                │
                              [AND]
                 ┌──────────────┴──────────────┐
        Live conductor exposed          Worker contacts it
                 │                              │
               [OR]                           [OR]
        ┌────────┴────────┐          ┌──────────┴──────────┐
   Damaged        No maintenance   No PPE          No isolation/LOTO
   insulation      inspection      (gloves)         procedure
```
**Where FTA is most useful:** nuclear power, aviation & aerospace, oil & gas / petrochemical, chemical process plants, rail, medical devices, software-safety-critical systems — i.e. **high-consequence, low-frequency, complex systems.**
