# Lecture 5 — Physical Hazards: Fire, Explosion & Toxicity

⏱ Study time: 60 min | 🔥 Exam weight: **HIGH** (Q4a — 6 marks, Q4b — 9 marks come straight from here)

## 1. Physical Hazards — definition & scope

**Definition:** potential risks capable of causing physical harm or injury, stemming from **physical agents, factors or conditions** in the work environment. They can harm **with or without direct contact**.
**OSHA:** a physical hazard is a condition that can lead to **death or serious physical damage**.

**Scope:** noise · vibration · radiation (ionizing & non-ionizing) · extreme temperatures (heat & cold) · electricity · confined spaces · body stressing · working at heights · vehicle operations · gases under pressure · inwardly converging surfaces.

**Why workplace safety matters:** protecting workers (prevent injury, illness, fatality) · **compliance** with OSHA (US) / HSE (UK) — 📌 **OSHA's General Duty Clause: employers must provide a workplace free from recognised hazards likely to cause serious harm or death** · business benefits (productivity, morale, retention, risk management).

### Common physical hazards summary table (learn this grid)
| Category | Examples | Harm |
|---|---|---|
| **Noise** | Loud machinery, impact tools, background noise | Hearing loss (temporary/permanent), tinnitus, stress, communication interference |
| **Vibration** | Drills, jackhammers, heavy machinery, vehicles | **Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome (HAVS)**, whole-body vibration, musculoskeletal & nerve damage |
| **Radiation** | X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive materials, UV, microwaves | Tissue damage, burns, cancer, genetic mutations, cataracts |
| **Mechanical** | Unguarded machinery, pinch/nip points, fast-moving equipment, falling objects | Crushing, lacerations, **amputations**, impact injuries |
| **Confined spaces** | Manholes, tanks, silos, pits, ducts, boilers | Asphyxiation (O₂ deficiency/excess), toxic gas, engulfment, fire/explosion, entrapment |
| **Temperature extremes** | Furnaces, outdoor sun / freezers, outdoor winter | Heatstroke, heat exhaustion, dehydration / hypothermia, frostbite, trench foot, chilblains |
| **Pressure deviations** | Pressure vessels, compressed gas cylinders, high altitude, diving | Explosions, ruptures, barotrauma, decompression sickness, hypoxia |
| **Electricity** | Frayed cords, exposed wiring, poor grounding, overloaded circuits | Electrocution, shocks, burns, fires |

## 2. Combustion: Fire Triangle & Fire Tetrahedron ⭐

### Fire Triangle (simpler model) — 3 elements
1. **Fuel** — any combustible material (wood, paper)
2. **Oxygen** — an oxidiser, typically from air
3. **Heat** — energy raising the fuel to its ignition point

👉 **Principle: remove any ONE of the three and combustion stops.**

### Fire Tetrahedron (more complete) — adds a 4th
4. **Chemical Chain Reaction** — the uninhibited self-sustaining reaction that perpetuates flaming combustion once started

👉 **All four must be present for flaming combustion.** Modern firefighting uses agents (dry chemicals) that **interrupt the chain reaction**.

| Component | Role in fire | Suppression method |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel** | Source of energy | **Fuel removal** (shut off the gas supply) |
| **Heat** | Initiates & sustains combustion | **Cooling** (water) |
| **Oxygen** (~21% of air) | Supports combustion | **Smothering** (CO₂, foam) |
| **Chemical chain reaction** | Generates extra heat, spreads fire | **Chemical inhibition** (dry chemical powder) |

### Ignition sources
Any form of energy capable of initiating combustion: **electrical equipment · static electricity · hot surfaces · chemicals and flammable liquids · mechanical sparks/friction · open flames & smoking · human error**.
**Prevention:** regular maintenance of equipment and electrical devices · fire safety training · proper storage of combustibles away from heat · effective process safety management.

## 3. ⭐⭐ The 5 Classes of Fire (near-guaranteed exam question)

| Class | Fuel type | Primary extinguishing method | Recommended extinguisher |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles — wood, paper, fabric, plastics | **Heat reduction (cooling)** | Water, ABC/powder, water mist, foam |
| **B** | Flammable liquids & gases — gasoline, alcohol, propane | Oxygen reduction (smothering) / chemical inhibition | ABC/powder, **CO₂**, water mist, clean agent |
| **C** | **Energized electrical** equipment — appliances, wiring, electronics | Oxygen reduction / chemical inhibition — must be **non-conductive** | ABC/powder, **CO₂**, water mist, clean agent |
| **D** | Combustible metals — titanium, magnesium, sodium | Oxygen reduction (smothering) | **Dry powder** (special, class-D only) |
| **K** | Cooking oils & fats — grease, vegetable & animal fats | Smothering / **saponification** | **Wet chemical**, water mist |

🧠 Mnemonic: **A**shes · **B**arrels (liquids) · **C**urrent (electric) · **D**ynamic metals · **K**itchen.
⚠️ **Never use water on Class C (electrocution) or Class D/K.** Using the wrong agent can be ineffective **or dangerous**.

## 4. Combustible Dust & the Explosion Pentagon ⭐

**Combustible dust:** any finely divided solid material (typically **420 microns or smaller**) that poses a fire/explosion hazard when dispersed in air.
- Materials: flour, sugar, plastics, wood, some metals (aluminium, magnesium)
- Industries at risk: **food processing, woodworking, pharmaceuticals, metal mining**

### The Explosion Pentagon — 5 elements, all simultaneously
= Fire triangle **+ 2 more**:
1. **Fuel** — the combustible dust
2. **Ignition source** — sufficient heat
3. **Oxygen** — oxidiser (air)
4. **Dispersion** — dust suspended as a cloud at sufficient concentration
5. **Confinement** — the cloud is in an enclosed/semi-enclosed space

**Primary vs secondary explosions:** a **primary** explosion inside equipment dislodges settled dust throughout the facility; that newly dispersed dust fuels a **much larger and more destructive secondary explosion**. *(This is what makes dust explosions catastrophic.)*

**Controls for combustible dust:**
- **Facility dust hazard assessment** — identify dusts, accumulation areas, ignition sources
- **Dust control** — dust collection systems, surfaces designed to minimise accumulation, regular cleaning with **approved vacuums**
- **Ignition control** — appropriate electrical equipment, **bonding & grounding** for static, manage open flames, maintain equipment
- **Damage control** — **deflagration venting**, spark/ember detection, explosion protection systems
- **Training** — hazard recognition, safe work practices, emergency procedures

## 5. Toxicity ⭐ (Q4a — "define toxicity and explain its significance" — 6 marks)

**Definition:** Toxicity describes the **types of effects a substance can cause and its potency (strength)**.

**Key principle:** a chemical only causes harm **if it enters or contacts the body**; the **physical form** (dust, fume, mist, vapour, gas, liquid) influences the hazard.

**Significance in workplace safety (write these points for the 6 marks):**
1. Determines **how hazardous** a substance is and at what dose — underpins exposure limits (PEL/TLV/REL)
2. Guides **hazard communication** — labelling, **SDS**, classification
3. Drives selection of **control measures** and the right PPE (e.g. correct respirator cartridge)
4. Essential for **exposure monitoring** and health surveillance targets
5. Supports **emergency planning** — spill response, first aid, antidotes
6. Basis for **legal compliance** and prevention of occupational disease (a highly toxic substance may require elimination/substitution rather than PPE)
7. Enables **risk assessment**: risk = toxicity (severity) × exposure (likelihood) — a toxic substance sealed in a closed system may be low risk; a mildly toxic one inhaled daily may be high risk

**Hazardous dusts:** fine solid particles suspended in air.
- *Generation:* mechanical processes — grinding, cutting, crushing, sanding
- *Sources:* sawdust, **silica** dust, coal dust, **asbestos** dust, metalliferous dust

### Routes of exposure (3)
1. **Inhalation (breathing)** — **the most common route** for dusts, fumes, mists; irritates the respiratory system or is absorbed into the bloodstream
2. **Ingestion (swallowing)** — chemicals settling on food, drink or hands
3. **Direct contact (skin/eyes)** — some substances are absorbed through skin

### Health effects (primarily respiratory)
- **Irritation** — eyes, nose, throat; shortness of breath; dermatitis
- **Reduced lung capacity** from chronic/prolonged exposure
- **Allergic reactions** — e.g. occupational asthma
- **Specific lung diseases** — silicosis, asbestosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis, mesothelioma

## 6. ⭐⭐ Q4b — Hierarchy of Controls for Toxic Exposures (9 marks)

> Structure your 9-mark answer as: **(1) state the principle → (2) engineering → (3) administrative → (4) PPE → (5) conclude.**

**Principle:** Prioritise engineering and work-practice controls; **PPE is the last resort**, because higher-level controls protect *everyone* passively and do not depend on worker behaviour or correct fit. (Full hierarchy: Elimination → Substitution → Engineering → Administrative → PPE.)

### Engineering controls (MOST effective)
- **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV)** — captures contaminants **at their source** before they enter the breathing zone
- **Wet methods** — apply water/liquid to suppress dust generation (e.g. wet cutting of concrete for silica)
- **Isolation or enclosure** — enclose the process to contain contaminants (glove boxes, sealed transfer)
- **Dust collection systems** — ducting and filters to capture airborne particles
- *(also: general dilution ventilation, automation/remote handling, elimination/substitution of the toxic agent)*

### Administrative controls
- **Work practices** — job rotation, adjusted schedules to limit exposure time
- **Housekeeping** — regular cleaning to prevent dust accumulation
- **Hazard communication** — labels, **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)**, worker training
- *(also: permits, restricted access zones, hygiene facilities — wash before eating, no eating in work areas, health surveillance)*

### PPE (last line of defence)
- Used **when other controls are not feasible or while they are being implemented**
- **Respirators** — must be **NIOSH-approved for the specific contaminant** and **properly fitted** (fit-testing, maintenance, training)
- **Gloves, eye protection and protective clothing** to prevent skin and eye contact
- Requires a written programme: selection, training, fit-testing, inspection, maintenance, replacement

**Concluding sentence:** *"Effective toxic-exposure management uses layered controls: engineering measures reduce the hazard at source, administrative controls limit exposure duration and inform workers, and PPE protects the individual when residual risk remains."*
