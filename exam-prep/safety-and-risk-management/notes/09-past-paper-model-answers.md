## QUESTION 1 (25 marks) — Case Study: the back-injured nurse

### 1(a)(i) Four ergonomic risk factors observed (4 marks — 1 each)

1. **Manual handling of heavy loads** — repeatedly lifting, pulling and physically carrying acutely ill, largely immobile elderly patients; patient weight far exceeds safe manual lifting limits (NIOSH equation).
2. **Repetitive tasks / cumulative trauma** — the same lifting, turning and transferring motions were performed over **ten years**, producing a *cumulative trauma spinal injury* rather than one acute event.
3. **Awkward postures** — bending, twisting and reaching across beds to turn patients side-to-side, sit them up, or transfer them to a chair or bedside commode, placing asymmetric load on the lumbar spine.
4. **Unpredictable/dynamic loads (sudden exertion)** — confused patients **resisted**, causing sudden unexpected forces and increased strain — the load is live and non-cooperative, unlike a box.

*(Bonus factors if you need a fifth: prolonged standing on 12-hour shifts; whole-body **vibration** and sustained seated posture while driving 5,000 miles to appointments; no mechanical assistive devices provided.)*

### 1(a)(ii) The process the hospital should follow to control ergonomic factors (8 marks)

Answer as a **structured ergonomic assessment + hierarchy of controls** process:

**Step 1 — Review existing data (1)**
Analyse workplace injury and illness records, workers' compensation claims, absence data and near-misses to identify high-risk units (medical/surgical, telemetry, intermediate care) and high-risk tasks.

**Step 2 — Gather subjective data (1)**
Conduct **workplace walk-throughs** on each ward and collect **employee feedback** — interviews and surveys with nurses about which tasks hurt, when, and why.

**Step 3 — Collect objective data using ergonomic assessment tools (2)**
Apply the recognised instruments: **WISHA Checklist** (caution-zone screening), the **NIOSH Lifting Equation** (recommended weight limit for lifts), **REBA** (Rapid Entire Body Assessment — whole body) and **RULA** (Rapid Upper Limb Assessment) to quantify postural load and lifting risk per task.

**Step 4 — Analyse and prioritise (1)**
Combine the data to identify key insights, rank the risks by likelihood × severity on a **risk matrix**, and target the highest-risk tasks first.

**Step 5 — Implement controls using the hierarchy of controls (2)**
- **Elimination:** a **zero-lift / no manual lift policy** — no nurse manually lifts a patient
- **Substitution:** replace manual transfers with mechanical transfers
- **Engineering:** ceiling-mounted and mobile **patient hoists**, slide sheets, transfer boards, sit-to-stand aids, electric height-adjustable beds, bariatric equipment
- **Administrative:** **dedicated lift teams**, safe patient handling training, two-person handling rules, job rotation, adequate staffing, pre-assessment of each patient's mobility, **permanent modified light duty for injured clinicians**
- **PPE:** non-slip footwear, gait belts (note: back belts are **not** a substitute for engineering controls)

**Step 6 — Train and communicate (0.5)**
Mandatory, refreshed training on safe patient handling, use of the devices, and early symptom reporting.

**Step 7 — Monitor, review and confirm effectiveness (0.5)**
Track MSD incidence, near-misses and equipment usage; re-assess after changes; audit compliance; feed results back into the next review cycle (**PDCA**). Provide **health surveillance and early rehabilitation/return-to-work support**.

### 1(a)(iii) How a strong safety culture could have prevented the injury (8 marks)

**Opening definition (1):** Safety culture is the **shared values, beliefs and attitudes** that determine safety behaviour — *"how we do things around here."* A strong culture is **proactive rather than reactive** and involves everyone from top management to frontline staff.

Then take each element and apply it to the case (about 1 mark each):

| Element | Relevance in this healthcare case |
|---|---|
| **Leadership commitment** | Management would have funded hoists, lift teams and safe-patient-handling programmes *before* injuries occurred, instead of leaving nurses to lift manually for ten years. Visible commitment signals that patient throughput never outranks staff safety. |
| **Employee participation** | Nurses know which transfers are dangerous. Safety committees, **JSA** of transfer tasks and hazard reporting would have surfaced the risk early and shaped practical solutions. |
| **Effective communication** | Two-way, open communication would have let the nurse report early back pain without fear; hazard information and safe handling procedures would be clearly and consistently shared across shifts. |
| **Continuous learning** | Investigating earlier back complaints and near-misses as **learning opportunities** would have revealed the systemic cause instead of treating each strain as an individual's bad luck. |
| **Accountability** | Clear responsibility at every level for safe handling — ward managers accountable for equipment availability and compliance, not just individual nurses. |
| **Mutual trust** | The nurse fought **years** through workers' compensation and had to "fight hard" for modified light duty — evidence of a *low-trust, blame-oriented* culture. In a high-trust culture, reporting and adjustment are supported, not contested. |
| **Adaptability** | The organisation would have adapted practice as evidence on healthcare MSDs and zero-lift policies emerged, rather than continuing a known-hazardous method. |

**Concluding sentence (1):** A strong safety culture converts the ergonomic hazard from an accepted feature of nursing into an unacceptable, actively controlled risk — producing zero-lift policies, lift teams, early reporting and permanent modified duty, which is precisely what the nurse advocates at the end of the case.

### 1(b) Why hazard management matters to employers — 5 benefits (5 marks)

1. **Reduces the risk of accidents and injuries** — proactive identification and control minimises workplace dangers before harm occurs.
2. **Improves efficiency and productivity** — fewer accidents mean less work disruption, downtime and absenteeism.
3. **Boosts employee relations and morale** — a safer environment increases job satisfaction, trust and retention.
4. **Lowers costs associated with accidents** — reduces healthcare/compensation expenses, lost productivity and legal liabilities (indirect costs are typically **2–4×** direct costs).
5. **Ensures compliance with legal and regulatory requirements** — avoids fines, prosecution and prohibition notices, and maintains a positive brand image.

## QUESTION 2 (25 marks)

### 2(a) Define hazard and risk with workplace examples (4 marks)
- **Hazard** = *any source with the potential to cause damage, harm or adverse health effects to someone or something.* It is the **source of harm** itself. Hazards may be physical conditions (slippery floor, loud noise), human predispositions (fatigue, lack of training), work methodologies (unsafe lifting technique), human actions or behavioural factors.
  *Example:* a **bottle of concentrated hydrochloric acid** in a laboratory; an **unguarded circular saw** in a joinery workshop.
- **Risk** = per **ISO 31000**, *"the effect of uncertainty on objectives"*; in OHS, the **combination of the likelihood of a hazardous event and the severity of the resulting harm** → **Risk = Likelihood × Severity**.
  *Example:* the *probability that a technician suffers chemical burns while decanting the acid, and how serious those burns would be*; the *probability and severity of finger amputation from the unguarded saw*.
- **Distinction to state explicitly:** the hazard exists whether or not anyone is exposed; the risk depends on exposure, frequency, number of people and the effectiveness of existing controls. Removing the hazard eliminates the risk; controls only reduce it.

### 2(b) Hazards in the Service, Retail and Hospitality sector in Sri Lanka (4 marks)
1. **Physical hazards** — slips, trips and falls in customer and work areas (wet floors, spillages, cables, cluttered aisles); **manual handling** injuries from lifting and moving stock.
2. **Psychosocial hazards** — stress, anxiety and depression arising from constant customer interaction and heavy workload; **difficult or aggressive customers** leading to abuse or violence; long and unpredictable hours causing fatigue.
3. **Workplace violence, bullying and harassment** — from customers, colleagues or management.
4. **Food service hazards** — cuts from knives, burns from hot surfaces/oil/steam, and chemical exposure from cleaning agents; food hygiene and biological contamination risks.
5. *(Contextual factors)* high employee **turnover** eroding safety training, and **limited resources** for safety management in the many small and informal businesses that dominate the Sri Lankan hospitality and retail trade.

### 2(c) Main objectives of workplace inspections — list 4 (4 marks)
1. To **proactively identify hazards** — both existing and potential — before they cause injury.
2. To **uncover the root causes** of identified hazards.
3. To **recommend corrective actions** to eliminate or control those hazards.
4. To **evaluate the effectiveness of existing risk control measures**.
5. *(also)* To **verify compliance** with safety standards, legislation and internal procedures, and to document findings for accountability and trend analysis.

### 2(d) Systematic procedure for an effective workplace inspection (5 marks)
1. **Plan and prepare** — define the scope, area and frequency; review previous inspection reports, incident and near-miss records, SDS and equipment manuals.
2. **Develop a customised checklist** — tailored to the specific environment, equipment and work processes, ensuring thoroughness and consistency.
3. **Involve workers** — include employee representatives, because workers have direct knowledge of the tasks and risks; this materially improves effectiveness.
4. **Conduct the inspection walkthrough** — systematically observe operations, equipment and facilities; look for both **unsafe conditions and unsafe acts**; interview operators; take photographs.
5. **Document findings** — record every hazard with location and a risk rating (likelihood × severity).
6. **Recommend and assign corrective actions** — each with a responsible person and a target completion date, prioritised using the hierarchy of controls.
7. **Communicate results** — report to management and workers.
8. **Follow up and re-inspect** — verify the actions were implemented and are effective; track progress and trends over time (accountability and continuous improvement).

### 2(e) Three accident causation theories + two real-world examples (8 marks)

**1. Domino Theory (Heinrich).** Accidents are a **sequence of five falling dominoes**: (1) ancestry and social environment → (2) fault of person → (3) **unsafe act or mechanical/physical hazard** → (4) accident → (5) injury. Removing any one domino — especially the third — prevents the injury.

**2. Human Factors Theory.** Accidents are caused by **human error**, which itself arises from **overload** (physical or mental limits exceeded), **incompatible workstation design** (poor ergonomics) and **stress**. Prevention means designing systems and tasks that account for human limitations rather than blaming the worker.

**3. Systems Theory.** Accidents result from **interactions within a complex system** — the interconnectedness of people, equipment, environment and management. Accidents are **system failures**, not merely individual actions, so the whole system must be analysed to find root causes.

*(4th if asked: **Energy Release Theory** — accidents are the uncontrolled release of energy (kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical) at a rate or amount exceeding the body's tolerance; prevention means controlling energy sources and placing barriers between energy and people.)*

**Real-world example 1 — Bhopal Gas Tragedy (India, 1984).** A methyl isocyanate leak from the Union Carbide plant killed thousands. **Systems Theory** explains it best: refrigeration units switched off to save cost, failed scrubbers and flare tower, understaffing, degraded maintenance, inadequate alarms and dense unplanned settlement around the plant — no single unsafe act, but a whole system of technical, managerial and regulatory failures interacting.

**Real-world example 2 — Piper Alpha (North Sea, 1988).** 167 died after a pump was restarted while its pressure safety valve had been removed for maintenance. Analysed by **Domino Theory**, the third domino is exactly the unsafe act/condition — the **permit-to-work failure and poor shift handover**; removing that one domino (a properly executed permit system) would have stopped the entire chain. **Human Factors Theory** also applies — shift-handover overload and communication breakdown.

*(A Sri Lankan/local alternative: a garment factory worker's hand caught in an unguarded machine — Domino: the missing guard is domino 3; Energy Release: uncontrolled kinetic energy of the moving part.)*

## QUESTION 3 (25 marks)

### 3(a) Concept of acceptable risk (2 marks)
Acceptable risk is the level of risk an organisation or society is **willing to tolerate** given the **benefits** derived from the activity and the **cost of further risk reduction**. It can also be expressed as the likelihood of suffering disease or injury that an individual, group or society will tolerate — determined by scientific data, social norms, economic conditions and legal frameworks. It must fall within the organisation's defined **risk appetite and risk tolerance**, and represents the **residual risk** remaining after all reasonably practicable controls are applied.

### 3(b) Five challenges in determining acceptable risk (5 marks)
1. **Subjectivity of risk perception** — different individuals and groups judge the same risk very differently.
2. **Difficulty quantifying certain risks**, particularly **long-term/chronic health effects** with long latency (e.g. asbestos, noise-induced hearing loss).
3. **Balancing competing priorities** — production demands, cost and deadlines versus safety.
4. **Uncertainty and limited knowledge** about hazards, especially novel technologies and materials.
5. **The dynamic nature of risk and evolving societal values** — what was acceptable a decade ago may not be today.
6. *(also)* **Lack of clear public debate and consensus** on specific risk levels; and **external influences** such as media coverage and public opinion distorting priorities.

### 3(c)(i) ALARP Principle (4 marks)
**ALARP = As Low As Reasonably Practicable.** It requires that risk be reduced to a level that is as low as reasonably practicable by **balancing the level of risk against the time, effort and cost** required to reduce it further.
- Central to it is the **"grossly disproportionate" test**: further risk reduction measures are not required where their cost would be **grossly disproportionate** to the benefit gained — note it is *grossly*, not merely marginally, disproportionate, and the burden rises as the risk rises.
- ALARP determines what level of **residual risk** is acceptable after all reasonable steps have been taken.
- Risk is divided into **three regions**: the **intolerable region** (risk cannot be justified — the activity must stop or be fundamentally changed); the **ALARP / tolerable region** (risk is tolerated only if reduction is impracticable or grossly disproportionate in cost); and the **broadly acceptable region** (risk is negligible; maintain existing controls and monitor).
- ⚠️ Distinguish from **ALARA** ("As Low As Reasonably **Achievable**"), the equivalent principle used specifically in **radiation protection**.

### 3(c)(ii) Fault Tree Analysis (4 marks)
**FTA** is a **top-down, deductive** analytical method used in safety and reliability engineering to understand how systems can fail, to identify risk-reduction measures and to determine the probability of an accident or system failure.
- **Purpose:** diagnose root causes of failures; determine inherent risks and how to minimise them; estimate the frequency of safety breaches.
- **Principles:** based on **multi-causality**; uses a logic diagram to trace event branches; employs **Boolean logic** with **AND, OR and INHIBIT gates**; starts with an **undesired TOP event** and works **backwards** to its causes; can be applied **qualitatively and quantitatively**.
- **Construction:** (1) clearly define the undesired top event; (2) identify all potential causes and contributing factors; (3) draw the tree top-down using event symbols (basic, intermediate, undeveloped, conditional) and logic gates.
- **Interpretation:** *qualitatively* it reveals failure pathways and the combinations of events that cause the top event; *quantitatively*, probabilities are assigned to basic events and combined (AND → multiply; OR → add) to compute the top-event probability. Key concepts are **minimal cut sets** (smallest combinations of basic events that cause the top event) and **minimal path sets** (smallest sets that, if all succeed, prevent it).
- **Use:** high-consequence, complex systems — nuclear, aviation, oil & gas, chemical process, rail, medical devices.

### 3(d) Two key international OSH organisations and their roles (6 marks)

**1. International Labour Organization (ILO)** — a **specialised agency of the United Nations** that sets global labour standards, including OSH.
- Works through legally binding **Conventions** (binding on member states upon ratification) and non-binding **Recommendations**.
- Key OSH instruments: **C155 Occupational Safety and Health Convention (1981)** — requires a coherent **national OSH policy** and action at government and enterprise level; **C187 Promotional Framework for OSH Convention (2006)** — promotes a national **culture of prevention** and OSH management systems; **C081 Labour Inspection Convention (1947)** — enforcement through labour inspection; **C161 Occupational Health Services Convention (1985)**.
- Ratified Conventions must be implemented through national legislation; even unratified standards influence collective agreements and case law. In **June 2022 the ILO recognised "a safe and healthy working environment" as a Fundamental Principle and Right at Work.** It also provides technical assistance to member states (e.g. the **"Safety + Health for All" Programme**, which supports Sri Lanka).

**2. International Organization for Standardization (ISO)** — develops **voluntary international standards**, most importantly **ISO 45001:2018**, the international standard for **Occupational Health and Safety Management Systems**.
- Published 2018, replacing **OHSAS 18001**; applicable to any organisation of any size or sector; uses the ISO **High-Level Structure**, so it integrates with **ISO 9001** (quality) and **ISO 14001** (environment).
- Built on the **Plan-Do-Check-Act** cycle and requires leadership commitment, worker involvement, hazard identification, risk evaluation, legal compliance, emergency preparedness and incident investigation.
- Crucially, **ISO 45001 has no legal status and does not replace national law** — it is an "add-on" that raises OSH standards above the legal minimum.

*(Other acceptable organisations: **WHO** — defines health and sets global health guidance; **OSHA** (US) and **HSE** (UK) — national regulators and standard-setters whose standards are used internationally; **NIOSH** — research and recommended exposure limits.)*

### 3(e) The Factories Ordinance of 1942 and its importance (4 marks)
The **Factories Ordinance No. 45 of 1942** is the **primary legislation governing occupational safety and health in Sri Lanka**.
- **Importance:** it **predates many modern global OSH frameworks**, signifying an early national commitment to worker protection; it establishes the statutory duty of employers to provide safe and healthy working conditions and provides the legal basis for inspection and enforcement by the **Department of Labour**.
- **Coverage:** originally factory environments; amendments extended it to **construction works, docks, wharves, quays, warehouses and ships**.
- **Health provisions (Part II):** cleanliness (s.6), overcrowding (s.7), temperature (s.11), ventilation (s.12), lighting (s.13), drainage of floors (s.14), sanitary conveniences (s.15) and medical supervision (s.16).
- **Welfare provisions (Part IV):** drinking water (s.46), washing facilities (s.47), accommodation for clothing (s.48), facilities for resting for female workers (s.49) and first aid (s.50).
- **Limitations (say this for the top marks):** it is now **outdated**, with many required regulations still pending and **significant gaps — notably no specific law on chemical safety or ergonomics** — and it does not adequately cover SMEs, the informal sector or modern psychosocial and technological hazards.

## QUESTION 4 (25 marks)

### 4(a) Define toxicity and explain its significance in workplace safety (6 marks)
**Definition:** Toxicity describes the **types of effects a substance can cause and its potency (strength)**. A chemical only causes harm **if it enters or contacts the body**, and the **physical form** of the substance (dust, fume, mist, vapour, gas, liquid) strongly influences the hazard it presents.

**Significance in workplace safety:**
1. **Determines the severity component of risk** — risk = toxicity (severity) × exposure (likelihood). A highly toxic substance in a sealed system may pose low risk, while a mildly toxic dust inhaled daily may pose high risk.
2. **Underpins occupational exposure limits** — PELs, TLVs and RELs are derived from toxicological data and set the legal/recommended ceilings for exposure.
3. **Drives hazard communication** — classification, labelling and **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)**, so workers know what they are handling.
4. **Guides selection of control measures** — a highly toxic agent demands **elimination or substitution** and engineering controls such as **LEV** or full enclosure, not merely PPE.
5. **Determines correct PPE** — the specific contaminant and its toxicity dictate the respirator type and cartridge, glove material and eye/skin protection.
6. **Directs exposure monitoring and health surveillance** — which agents to sample, by which route (inhalation, ingestion, dermal), and which biological effects to screen for.
7. **Supports emergency planning** — spill response, first aid, antidotes and evacuation criteria.
8. **Prevents occupational disease and ensures legal compliance** — long-latency diseases such as silicosis, asbestosis and occupational asthma arise directly from unmanaged toxic exposure.

### 4(b) Hierarchy of controls for managing toxic exposures (9 marks)
**Principle (1 mark):** Controls are ranked from most to least effective — **Elimination → Substitution → Engineering controls → Administrative controls → PPE**. Higher-level controls are prioritised wherever reasonably practicable because they protect everyone passively, independent of worker behaviour, training or correct fit. **PPE is the last line of defence.**

**Engineering controls — most effective (3 marks):**
- **Local Exhaust Ventilation (LEV):** captures contaminants **at their source** before they reach the worker's breathing zone (e.g. fume hood over a soldering bench, capture hood on a grinding station).
- **Wet methods:** applying water or other liquids to suppress dust generation (e.g. wet cutting of concrete to control **respirable crystalline silica**).
- **Isolation or enclosure:** fully enclosing the process to contain contaminants (glove boxes, sealed reactors, closed chemical transfer systems), or isolating the worker in a positive-pressure control booth.
- **Dust collection systems:** ducted extraction with appropriate filters to capture airborne particles.
- *(Also at this level: elimination of the toxic substance entirely, or substitution with a less toxic alternative — e.g. water-based instead of solvent-based paint; automation/remote handling; general dilution ventilation.)*

**Administrative controls (3 marks):**
- **Safe work practices:** job rotation and adjusted schedules to limit each individual's exposure duration; restricting the number of workers in the exposure zone; scheduling high-emission tasks outside peak occupancy.
- **Housekeeping:** regular, thorough cleaning to prevent dust accumulation and re-entrainment — using approved vacuums rather than dry sweeping or compressed air.
- **Hazard communication and training:** labels, **Safety Data Sheets (SDS)** and worker training on hazards, routes of exposure, safe handling and emergency procedures.
- *(Also: permit-to-work systems, restricted access zones and signage, hygiene facilities and rules — wash before eating, no eating or drinking in work areas — plus exposure monitoring and health surveillance.)*

**Personal Protective Equipment — last line of defence (2 marks):**
- Used **only when other controls are not feasible, or as an interim measure while they are being implemented**, and as a supplement to them.
- **Respirators:** must be **NIOSH-approved for the specific contaminant**, correctly selected for the exposure level and properly **fit-tested**, worn, inspected and maintained under a written respiratory protection programme.
- **Gloves, eye protection and protective clothing** appropriate to the chemical, to prevent skin and eye contact and absorption.
- Effectiveness depends entirely on correct selection, fit, consistent use, training and maintenance — which is precisely why it ranks last.

**Conclusion:** Toxic exposures are best managed through a **layered defence** — engineering controls reduce the hazard at source, administrative controls limit exposure duration and inform workers, and PPE protects the individual against the residual risk. The controls must then be **monitored, reviewed and confirmed effective** (the *Confirmation* principle of occupational health).

### 4(c) Any four components of the COSO ERM 2017 framework (4 marks)
1. **Governance and Culture** — board risk oversight, operating structures, defined desired culture, core values and commitment to competence; establishes the tone at the top.
2. **Strategy and Objective-Setting** — analysing the business context, defining **risk appetite**, evaluating alternative strategies and formulating business objectives aligned with that appetite.
3. **Performance** — identifying, assessing the severity of, and prioritising risks; implementing risk responses; developing a **portfolio view** of risk.
4. **Review and Revision** — assessing substantial change, reviewing risk and performance, and pursuing improvement in enterprise risk management.
5. **Information, Communication and Reporting** — leveraging information systems, communicating risk information, and reporting on risk, culture and performance.

### 4(d) The three key components of ISO 31000 (6 marks)
**1. Principles (2 marks).** The **foundation** of effective risk management and the basis for creating and protecting value. They state that risk management should: create and protect value; be an **integral part of organisational processes** and of **decision-making**; explicitly address **uncertainty**; be **systematic, structured and timely**; be based on the **best available information**; be **tailored** to the organisation; take **human and cultural factors** into account; be **transparent and inclusive**; be **dynamic, iterative and responsive to change**; and facilitate **continual improvement**.

**2. Framework (2 marks).** The set of components providing the **foundations and organisational arrangements** for designing, implementing, monitoring, reviewing and continually improving risk management **throughout the organisation**. It secures **leadership and commitment**, and ensures risk management is **integrated** into governance, strategy, planning, reporting, policies, values and culture — i.e. it answers *who is responsible and how risk management is organised and sustained*.

**3. Process (2 marks).** The **systematic application** of management policies, procedures and practices to the activities of **communicating and consulting; establishing the context; risk identification; risk analysis; risk evaluation** (these three together forming **risk assessment**); **risk treatment**; and **monitoring and review**. It is **iterative** — the output of monitoring feeds back into every earlier stage.
