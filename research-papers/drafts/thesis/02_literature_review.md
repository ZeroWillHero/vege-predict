# Chapter 2: Literature Review

## 2.1 Overview

This chapter reviews prior work on agricultural and vegetable price forecasting, organized from classical statistical models through machine learning and deep learning approaches to hybrid statistical–ML methods and multi-source, Sri Lanka-specific studies. It closes by identifying the specific gap that motivates this research: the absence, in the existing literature, of a system that combines multi-source data fusion, forecasting, explainability, and farmer-facing advisory generation in one pipeline. The papers reviewed here are held in `research-papers/references/`.

## 2.2 Statistical Time-Series Models

ARIMA and its seasonal extension SARIMA/SARIMAX remain the standard baseline in agricultural price forecasting because they are simple to fit and interpret, and they capture trend and seasonality well when a series is close to linear. Weerasekara et al. (2026) note that Sri Lankan vegetable price research has historically been confined to ARIMA and GARCH-family models applied to single markets — citing, for instance, a SARIMA(3,1,2)(0,0,2)[52] model achieving 71% forecast accuracy for carrot retail prices. Mayank, Shelke, and Roy (2025) similarly describe ARIMA/SARIMA as effective for linear trends in Indian agri-horticultural commodities but limited once volatility becomes nonlinear. Across the literature, the recurring criticism is the same: these models assume linearity and struggle when prices are driven by irregular shocks — exactly the pattern Sri Lanka's import-isolated vegetable market produces. This motivates SARIMAX's role in the current project as the linear/seasonal baseline component rather than the sole forecasting model.

## 2.3 Machine Learning Approaches

Tree-based and kernel methods have repeatedly outperformed classical statistical baselines once nonlinear, multi-feature data is available. Paul et al. (2022), forecasting wholesale brinjal prices across seventeen markets in Odisha, India, compared Generalized Regression Neural Networks (GRNN), Support Vector Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting Machines against ARIMA, and found GRNN performed best in most markets, with Random Forest close behind in several; ARIMA and the other ML methods trailed. This is directly relevant here since brinjal is one of the six vegetables targeted by this project. Madubhashini (2021), in the first Sri Lanka-specific machine learning study of vegetable prices, compared gradient boosting, XGBoost, random forest regression, and stacked regression on Central Bank daily price data (2016–2022) and selected the best-performing model per vegetable, noting that prior Sri Lankan work had been limited to traditional statistical techniques.

## 2.4 Deep Learning and Sequence Models

LSTM and its variants have become common where long-range temporal dependence matters. Mayank, Shelke, and Roy (2025) report that an attention-based LSTM outperformed plain LSTM, ARIMA, SVM, Random Forest, and XGBoost variants for onion, potato, and pulse prices in India, and that adding weather and market-arrival features improved every model's accuracy — evidence that exogenous features, not just model architecture, drive forecasting quality. Zhao et al. (2025) propose a hybrid combining Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) with XGBoost, arguing that TCN's convolutional structure captures short- and long-term dependencies more cheaply than a recurrent LSTM; on 65,750 rice, wheat, and corn price points, their TCN-XGBoost model (RMSE 0.26, MAPE 5.3%) outperformed standalone ARIMA (RMSE 0.36) and LSTM (RMSE 0.34), as well as other hybrids such as Transformer-XGBoost and CNN-XGBoost.

## 2.5 Hybrid Statistical–ML Models

The pattern across multiple papers is that hybridizing a linear statistical model with a nonlinear ML model — fitting the statistical model first and using ML to model its residuals or as a parallel ensemble member — consistently beats either component alone. Patil et al. (2023) propose a Hybrid SARIMA-LSTM (HySALS) for global commodity prices (wheat, millet, sorghum, maize, rice), pairing SARIMA's seasonal structure with LSTM's capacity for nonlinear residual patterns. Mayank, Shelke, and Roy (2025) report several related combinations in the literature — ARIMA-LSTM, HMM-LSTM, CNN-LSTM — each improving on its individual components for a different commodity or market. This directly supports the two-stage hybrid design already scaffolded in this project (SARIMAX fitted first, XGBoost or CatBoost trained on its residuals): it is a well-established pattern in the literature, not a novel risk.

## 2.6 Multi-Source and Exogenous Feature Integration

A second consistent finding is that adding exogenous, structurally meaningful features improves forecasts more than switching model architecture does. Shree Sanjay and Janarthanan (2025) build a vegetable price predictor around real-time weather API data (temperature, humidity, rainfall) rather than model sophistication. Weerasekara et al. (2026) go further: they construct an integrated Sri Lankan dataset (2013–2019) combining retail and farmer-gate prices with origin-zone weather, diesel costs, and USD/LKR exchange rates across 12 vegetable varieties and 14 market centres, and train separate gradient-boosted (XGBoost + LightGBM) models per cultivation season. Their season-specific model reached an R² of 0.9420 in the Yala season, while a unified model achieved 90.84% overall accuracy and, notably, retained 85.96% accuracy on the fully unseen 2024 hyperinflationary period without retraining — evidence that supply-chain-aware features generalize across economic regimes, not just within the training period. This is the closest prior work to the present project's data-fusion objective, though it stops at forecasting: it does not address explainability or farmer-facing advisory output.

## 2.7 Sri Lanka-Specific Studies

Four studies anchor this review to the Sri Lankan context directly, and together they trace how the field has moved from single-factor statistical models toward multi-feature, multi-market machine learning.

Ranaweera, Rathnayake, and Ananda (2023) provide the earliest dedicated machine learning study of Sri Lankan vegetable prices, and it is unusually well aligned with this project: they forecast beans, brinjal, carrot, and pumpkin (three of this project's six target vegetables) using four exogenous factors — rainfall, temperature, fuel price, and crop production — collected from the Central Bank, the Meteorological Department, the Department of Agriculture, and the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation across five economic centres (Dambulla, Thabuttegama, Nuwara Eliya, Narahenpita, Embilipitiya). Comparing Linear Regression, SMO Regression, Multilayer Perceptron, Random Forest, and M5P, they found Random Forest consistently most accurate across all four vegetables, with pumpkin the easiest to predict and carrot achieving MAE around 20 with Random Forest against 41+ for linear regression. Notably, this confirms the same four exogenous drivers (weather, fuel, production) already scoped into this project's `configs/config.yaml`.

Ruhunuge, Wijeratne, Esham, Fernando, and Kuruppu (2024) narrow the focus to a single vegetable — carrot — and a single mechanism: the causal effect of climate. Using 23 years (2000–2023) of HARTI wholesale carrot price data from Nuwara Eliya, they fit a Vector Autoregression (VAR) model with a lag order of 6 relating temperature, precipitation, and carrot prices, and confirm via Granger causality testing that precipitation changes significantly drive carrot price fluctuations. This is a useful complement to the ML-driven studies above: it isolates *why* weather matters (a causal, not just correlational, link) for the specific up-country crop this project also targets.

Madubhashini (2021/2023) established the first broad machine-learning comparison for Sri Lankan vegetable prices — gradient boosting, XGBoost, random forest regression, and stacked regression — on Central Bank daily price data (2016–2022), selecting the best-performing model per vegetable and explicitly noting that prior Sri Lankan work had been limited to ARIMA-style statistical techniques.

Weerasekara et al. (2026) is the most advanced of the four: a multi-market, multi-season, multi-feature system (XGBoost + LightGBM, Optuna-tuned) validated across a genuine economic regime shift (the unseen 2024 hyperinflationary period), achieving season-specific R² of 0.9420 and unified-model accuracy of 90.84%.

Across all four, the pattern is consistent with the broader literature (Sections 2.2–2.6): tree-based/gradient-boosted ML beats linear and single-factor statistical models, and weather, fuel, and production data are the exogenous features that matter most. But none of the four incorporates satellite crop-condition indices, IoT sensor data, or farmer behavioral input, and none includes an explainability or advisory layer.

## 2.8 Satellite and IoT Data in Agricultural Forecasting

None of the price-forecasting studies above (Sections 2.2–2.7) use satellite or IoT sensor data — the closest they come is farm-level production figures reported after the fact (Ranaweera et al., 2023). Evidence for the value of these two data sources instead comes from the adjacent crop-yield-prediction literature, which this project's objective 1 (multi-source fusion including Sentinel-2 NDVI and IoT readings) draws on directly.

Darra, Espejo-Garcia, Kasimati, Kriezi, Psomiadis, and Fountas (2023) predict processing tomato yield — a vegetable crop, structurally similar to this project's targets — from Sentinel-2 vegetation indices across 108 fields in Greece, comparing ensemble machine learning (AutoML-selected regressors) against classical statistical models; the ML ensemble outperformed the statistical baseline, and vegetation indices captured in-season crop vigor well enough to forecast yield ahead of harvest. This supports the underlying premise of adding NDVI as a feature here: satellite-observed crop condition carries forecasting-relevant signal beyond price history and weather alone. Ayall, Li, Beddows, Markovic, and Leontidis (2025) address a different obstacle — IoT sensor data is often incomplete, since sensors are only deployed for some growing seasons — and propose an AI-based "backcasting" method that synthesizes plausible historical sensor readings for seasons without coverage from historical weather data, showing that models trained on this combined real-plus-synthetic data outperform models trained on real sensor data alone. This is directly relevant should this project's own IoT integration (still deferred, per Chapter 1's scope) encounter incomplete sensor coverage across the study period.

Neither paper forecasts price, and neither is Sri Lanka-specific — they establish that satellite and IoT data carry usable signal for crop condition, not that this signal has been connected to price forecasting anywhere in the literature reviewed here.

## 2.9 Explainable AI and LLM-Based Advisory in Agricultural Forecasting

Chapter 1 identifies a "trust and usability gap": accurate forecasts that remain opaque numbers rather than something a farmer can verify and act on. This section reviews the two literatures that this project's objectives 3 and 4 (SHAP explainability, LLM advisory) draw on — neither of which had been sourced when this chapter was first drafted.

**Explainable AI applied to price forecasting.** Two recent papers apply SHAP directly to agricultural commodity price models, demonstrating that explainability and forecasting accuracy are not in tension. Patro, Nitnaware, Das, R, G, and Dash (2025) propose EXACT-FARM, a hybrid SARIMAX-XGBoost-LSTM architecture — SARIMAX for trend/seasonality, XGBoost for nonlinear residual structure, LSTM for temporal dependence, an ensemble structurally similar to this project's own SARIMAX-residual hybrids — paired with SHAP-based feature-level explanations and counterfactual ("what-if") analysis, reporting a 15–20% accuracy improvement over their non-hybrid baselines alongside the explanation layer. Jain, Lwin, Dal, and Oo (2026) test a broader model zoo (statistical, ML, deep learning, and hybrid) across 22 commodities and seven Indian states, apply SHAP to identify the dominant price drivers per commodity, and find Random Forest the strongest performer (RMSE 1.49, R² 0.9995) — the same model family this project's own results identify as the strongest performer on 5 of 6 vegetables (Chapter 4), a convergent finding from an independent dataset and market. Both papers confirm SHAP is a practical, non-accuracy-sacrificing choice for the explainability layer this project scopes into objective 3.

**LLM-based agricultural advisory.** Three papers ground the advisory side. Tzachor, Devare, Richards, Pypers, Ghosh, Koo, and King (2023), writing in *Nature Food*, argue that large language models can lower the cost of delivering personalized, localized agricultural guidance at a scale traditional extension services cannot reach, while cautioning that ungrounded LLM output risks generic or unsupported advice in a high-stakes domain — a caution directly relevant to this project's own advisory design, which grounds its LLM output in a specific forecast and SHAP explanation rather than open-ended generation. Sawant, Nair, and Hariharan (2026) build a retrieval-augmented-generation (RAG) advisory system over agricultural best-practice documents for five crops, evaluating several open LLMs (Llama 3.1, Mistral, Phi3, Qwen2.5) on retrieval quality and answer alignment; Samuel, Skarga-Bandurova, Sikolia, and Awais (2026) similarly combine RAG with a symbolic domain-knowledge layer (AgroLLM) that constrains retrieval and validates outputs against agronomic thresholds, reporting a 95.2% accuracy on a 504-question benchmark and a substantial reduction in hallucinated or numerically invalid advice compared to an unconstrained LLM. The consistent lesson across all three is that grounding an LLM's output in structured, verifiable domain data — not the LLM alone — is what makes farmer-facing advice trustworthy, directly informing this project's plan (Chapter 1, objective 4) to ground advisory generation in a specific forecast plus its SHAP explanation rather than an open-ended prompt.

None of these five papers is Sri Lanka-specific, none forecasts vegetable prices, and — critically — none of the explainability papers is paired with an LLM advisory layer, or vice versa: SHAP-explained price forecasting and LLM-based farmer advisory exist as two separate literatures that this project's objectives 3–4 are the first (among the papers reviewed here) to connect to each other and to a specific forecast.

## 2.10 MLOps, Automated Retraining, and Deployment

A separate literature thread, orthogonal to forecasting accuracy, concerns what happens to a model *after* it is trained: how it gets deployed, monitored, and kept current as new data arrives. This becomes directly relevant once the project moves from producing a one-off comparison of trained models (Chapters 3–4) toward an application that must keep forecasting as new weekly price/weather/fuel data arrives.

Garg et al. (2022) frame the general MLOps lifecycle around four practices — Continuous Integration (testing data and models, not just code), Continuous Delivery (automated deployment of a trained model), Continuous Training (automatically retraining models as new data arrives), and Continuous Monitoring (tracking production performance) — and note that, unlike conventional DevOps, ML pipelines must treat data and model versions as first-class artifacts alongside code. Meisenbacher et al. (2022), reviewing automated time-series forecasting pipelines specifically, find that most published pipelines automate only two or three of the five design stages (pre-processing, feature engineering, hyperparameter optimization, method selection, ensembling) and call for a more holistic treatment — a gap this project's pipeline (Chapter 3) already largely closes for the training side, but not yet for ongoing retraining.

The central open question in this thread is *when* to retrain. Two strategies recur across the literature: blind triggering, which retrains on a fixed schedule or after a fixed volume of new data regardless of whether performance has actually degraded, and informed triggering, which retrains only when a drift detector fires. Pham et al. (2024) distinguish data drift (the input distribution changes) from concept drift (the relationship between inputs and the target changes) and show that fully supervised drift detectors are impractical (they require labeling every new observation) while fully unsupervised ones miss concept drift that isn't accompanied by visible data drift — motivating semi-supervised detectors as a middle ground. Katalay, Dimandja, and Masakuna (2025) propose an automated MLOps pipeline that combines multiple statistical distribution-shift tests (Population Stability Index, KL divergence) to trigger retraining only when a shift is significant, explicitly to avoid the computational and cloud-cost waste of retraining on a fixed schedule regardless of need.

Two findings bear directly on this project's specific model families. First, XGBoost (and by extension the tree-based models used throughout this project — Random Forest, XGBoost, CatBoost) has no native incremental-learning mode: it assumes the full training set is available upfront, and naively updating a fitted model with only new observations violates that assumption and can produce unreliable results — meaning retraining these models means refitting from scratch on the full accumulated history, not an online update. Second, repeated SARIMA/SARIMAX refitting is explicitly reported as computationally burdensome in a rolling-window setting, consistent with what this project already found empirically (Chapter 3): a single SARIMAX fit with a 52-week seasonal period takes seconds to tens of seconds, and the two SARIMAX-based hybrids inherit that cost.

Two papers ground this in the agricultural-price domain directly. Jain, Marvaniya, Godbole, and Munigala (2020), forecasting tomato and maize prices across Indian marketplaces, propose a context-based model selection and retraining framework driven by model stability, data-quality metrics, and price-trend analysis — and report that manually-entered daily crop prices are themselves error-prone (missing entries, incorrect values), making data-quality monitoring a precondition for reliable retraining, not an afterthought. Zelingher (2025)'s AGRICAF system forecasts global agricultural commodity prices up to a year ahead by combining explainable ML with econometric methods, aimed explicitly at making forecasts accessible to non-specialist stakeholders (smallholder farmers, low-income-country policymakers) rather than only investors and large traders — directly aligned with this project's farmer-facing advisory objective (Chapter 1).

**Implication for this project's design**: given the modest scale here — a single machine, weekly (not streaming) data arrival, and a full 42-artifact retrain that already completes in roughly 10–15 minutes (Chapter 4) — a full drift-detection infrastructure of the kind built for cloud-scale classifier deployments (Katalay et al., 2025; Pham et al., 2024) would be disproportionate to the actual problem size. The literature above still points to the right *concerns* to design against — data-quality checks before training (Jain et al., 2020), a validation gate before promoting a newly retrained model to production (echoing the CI/CD "test the model, not just the code" principle from Garg et al., 2022), and honest acknowledgment that tree-based models require full refits, not incremental updates — without requiring statistical drift-detection machinery that this project's scale doesn't justify.

## 2.11 Research Gap

Synthesizing the above: the forecasting-accuracy problem for Sri Lankan vegetable prices is increasingly well addressed — recent gradient-boosted, season-aware, multi-feature models achieve strong and regime-robust accuracy, and the specific exogenous drivers (rainfall, temperature, fuel price, production) are already established in the Sri Lankan literature (Ranaweera et al., 2023; Ruhunuge et al., 2024). Explainability and LLM-based advisory are also no longer unaddressed *in general* — Section 2.9 (Explainable AI and LLM-Based Advisory) shows both now have a literature of their own: SHAP has been applied directly to agricultural commodity price models (Patro et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2026), and RAG-grounded LLM advisory systems exist for farmers (Tzachor et al., 2023; Sawant et al., 2026; Samuel et al., 2026). Similarly, satellite NDVI and IoT sensor data have demonstrated forecasting-relevant signal for vegetable crops specifically (Darra et al., 2023; Ayall et al., 2025), even though that signal has not yet been connected to price.

What remains genuinely absent — across every paper reviewed in this chapter, general or Sri Lanka-specific — is the *combination*: no paper fuses satellite, IoT, and farmer behavioral data alongside market and weather data for price forecasting; no paper pairs a price forecast's SHAP explanation with an LLM-generated advisory message (the explainability and advisory literatures remain two separate threads, per Section 2.9's closing observation); and none of this exists for the Sri Lankan vegetable market, where the specific exogenous drivers, growing-season structure, and import-isolation dynamics are already well characterized (Sections 2.6–2.7) but only as far as the forecast itself. This project's five objectives (Chapter 1) are designed to fill precisely this combination, building on the modeling, explainability, and advisory techniques validated independently across this chapter's literature rather than inventing any of them from scratch.
